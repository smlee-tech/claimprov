# Concepts

This document explains what __claimprov__ is for, the distinction it instantiates,
the design decisions that distinction forces, and the boundaries of what the
code claims. It is the place to read before using the library or contributing
to it.

claimprov is the companion implementation of a working paper in information
systems research on how interpretations generated by AI agents become
organizational data. The paper supplies the theory; this library supplies a
precise, executable version of its central distinction so that the argument
can be examined, reproduced and built on.

## 1. The phenomenon

Enterprise systems increasingly contain values that no human and no
design-time rule produced: a churn-risk score written into a CRM field by a
sales agent, a supplier classified as "potentially non-compliant" by a
procurement agent, a lead-time estimate summarized from order history by an
analysis agent. These are *interpretations*, produced at runtime, with
task-specific discretion over their content, form and destination.

What is new is not automated data production, which is as old as ETL. It is
the combination of three properties: the inscription itself (not only its
value) is determined at runtime; the arrangement can omit any human who would
translate or vet the output before reuse; and the primary consumer of the
output may be another computational agent. When those three hold, an
interpretation can travel from one agent to the next and be acted on without
anyone, human or machine, having assessed whether it was warranted.

## 2. Declared and enacted epistemic status

claimprov rests on one distinction.

**Declared epistemic status** is the qualification explicitly represented on
an informational artefact: observed fact, derived calculation, agent-generated
inference, provisional classification, human-validated assertion. It is encoded
in metadata, certification tags, provenance records or validation flags.

**Enacted epistemic status** is the standing the artefact acquires through
downstream treatment. The definition is behavioral: an artefact has the enacted
status of evidence when a downstream process uses it as a sufficient basis for
inference or action, so that consequences follow from it as if it were
warranted. This definition does not require a consumer with beliefs. An agent
that retrieves a provisional score and ranks accounts on it has enacted that
score as evidence, whether or not anything in the agent "believed" it.

The two can diverge, and the divergence is what this library makes
observable. claimprov therefore models retrieval as a first-class provenance
event (`cp:Retrieval`) with its own `cp:enactedStatus`. The W3C PROV model
records what happened to an entity; claimprov additionally records what the
consumer *did with* the entity's qualification. The conditions under which
declared and enacted status converge or diverge are the subject of the
companion paper and are not restated here.

## 3. Claims as the unit of governance

Enterprise governance usually certifies **assets**: a table, a semantic view,
a metric definition. Agent-generated interpretations arrive at a finer grain,
as a single **claim** written into or alongside an existing asset. claimprov
takes the claim as the unit of governance. Every proposition an agent puts
into circulation is a `cp:Claim` with its own declared status, attribution,
lineage, evidence and verifications. The library does not replace asset-level
governance; it supplies a representation at the grain at which agents
actually reason.

## 4. The status lattice and what it encodes

| status | trust rank | requires |
|---|---|---|
| `asserted` | 0 | attribution to an agent |
| `derived` | 0 | at least one source claim via `prov:wasDerivedFrom` |
| `tool-verified` | 1 | a passed `cp:Verification` by a `cp:Tool` (or a person) that cites evidence |
| `human-verified` | 2 | a passed `cp:Verification` by a `prov:Person` that cites evidence |

Two design decisions carry weight.

*Derivation is not verification.* `asserted` and `derived` share trust rank 0.
Deriving a claim from other claims adds lineage, which is valuable, but it
does not add warrant. Inference over verified inputs does not yield a verified
output. A claim that declares a higher rank than one of its sources without a
passed verification of its own has laundered its status through derivation,
and the SHACL shapes reject it.

*An LLM re-reading a claim is not verification.* Only a deterministic tool or
a person can perform a `cp:Verification` that raises a claim's rank. This
encodes the distinction between *authorization* (what an agent is permitted to
read and write) and *epistemic warrant* (whether what it produced is reliable
enough to act on). Passing through a tool boundary, or inheriting a source's
access protections, confers the first and not the second.

## 5. Two failure modes, two severities

The SHACL shapes distinguish two things that are often conflated.

**Storage-time violations.** A claim that declares a status without the
evidence that status requires is malformed and should not be stored. This
covers the per-status requirements above and status laundering across a
derivation chain. These are the failures studied in recent computer-science
work on provenance and status degradation inside agent pipelines
(e.g. PROV-AGENT, Souza et al. 2025).

**Retrieval-time divergence.** A consumer that enacts a higher status than the
claim declares has not produced malformed data. It has produced the
declared/enacted gap. claimprov reports this as a *warning*, not a violation,
because it is a measurement of the phenomenon rather than a defect in the
record. The warning is the library's main output.

Keeping these apart matters. A claim store can be perfectly well-formed and
still let every provisional inference be acted on as fact. Storage-time rules
and retrieval-time measurement answer different questions.

## 6. What the code does and does not claim

claimprov is a reference implementation of a theoretical distinction. It
shows that the distinction can be formalized precisely enough to validate, and
it provides an instrument with which an agent chain can be observed. It is
not:

- an empirical finding about any platform, vendor or organization;
- a detection product, a safety layer or a guarantee that agents will behave;
- an endorsement of any particular governance architecture.

The companion paper is conceptual; this library is the part of that work that
can be run.

## 7. Why the code is shared

Three reasons, in order of importance.

1. **Reproducibility of a conceptual argument.** The paper's worked
   configurations hold the input fixed and vary the information-system
   arrangement. Those configurations are reproducible here as small scripts,
   so a reader can check what the model predicts rather than take it on
   description.
2. **A shared vocabulary.** Claim-level epistemic status has no standard
   representation. By extending W3C PROV-O rather than inventing a new model,
   claimprov offers terms (`cp:Claim`, `cp:declaredStatus`, `cp:Retrieval`,
   `cp:enactedStatus`) that other provenance tooling can adopt or map to.
3. **An instrument for empirical work.** The behavioral definition of enacted
   status needs a way to record what a consumer did at retrieval. The
   `RetrievalEvent` and the divergence measure are that recording. Anyone
   studying an agent chain in a real deployment can instrument it with this
   schema.

## 8. Terminology

The library follows the paper's terminology, and contributions should too.

- *Epistemic status* or *standing*, not *trust*. Trust is a property of a
  relationship; status is a property of an artefact's treatment.
- *Persist*, not *inscribe*, unless actor-network theory is being cited.
- *Claim*, not *fact*, for anything an agent produces. Whether it functions
  as a fact is what enacted status records.
